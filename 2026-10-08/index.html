The bronze foundry in Prescott Valley
The bronze foundry in Prescott Valley
Tucked on East 2nd Street in Prescott Valley is a working fine art foundry, where sculptures are cast in bronze the old way, by the lost-wax process.
Bronzesmith opens it up for a tour every Thursday at 11:00 am, twenty dollars a person. They ask you to reserve ahead, and individuals and small groups are welcome. 7331 E. 2nd St., or call 928-772-2378.

🚗 Worth the Drive
Cathedral Rock from the banks of Oak Creek
Crescent Moon Picnic Site at Red Rock Crossing, about seven miles southwest of Sedona
Everybody has seen Cathedral Rock on a postcard. The best place to see it in person is from the creek, where the Forest Service picnic site puts the spires right over Oak Creek and, on a still day, reflected in it.
Come off State Route 89A onto Upper Red Rock Loop Road. Through November it is open 8:00 am to 6:00 pm. Day use is $15 a car for up to five people, with picnic tables, water and restrooms, and dogs welcome on a leash.
Pack lunch, wade if the water allows, and take the long way home.

📖 Worth the Read
The girl on the pony
A two-minute read
In 1882 a twelve-year-old rode a pony from Kansas to Arizona Territory, herding the horses her father meant to sell. The family settled on Lynx Creek, near where Prescott Valley sits today, and later called their place Orchard Ranch.
She had very little formal schooling. She became Arizona's territorial historian anyway, the first woman to hold a salaried office in the territory, and in 1928 she opened the old Governor's Mansion in Prescott as a museum for everything she had gathered.
Her name was Sharlot Hall. Since May there has been a nearly seven-foot bronze of her in the museum's rose garden, and this Friday morning the museum is free to walk into.
Read the full story on our blog
Source: Sharlot Hall Museum, “Who Was Sharlot Hall?”, and Days Past, “Celebrating Sharlot M. Hall” by Mary Melcher
💡 Last week's answer
Why is it called “Chino” Valley?
B. It refers to the curly grama grass growing there
Lt. Amiel W. Whipple gave the community its name. Chino was the Mexican name for the curly grama grass that grows in abundance across the area.
Source: Arizona Commerce Authority, Community Profile for Chino Valley
