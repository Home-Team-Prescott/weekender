🚗 Worth the Drive
Seligman, where Historic Route 66 begins
About an hour and a half north of Prescott Valley
Route 66 turns 100 this year, and Seligman is a fine place to tip your hat to it. When Interstate 40 bypassed the town in 1978, a local barber named Angel Delgadillo formed the Historic Route 66 Association of Arizona. In 1987 the State of Arizona recognized the road from Seligman to Kingman as Historic Route 66, and the town has called itself its birthplace ever since.
Today Seligman is the start of the longest surviving stretch of the old road, running 160 miles to Topock. Main street is a row of gift shops, diners and old cars, including Angel & Vilma Delgadillo’s Original Route 66 Gift Shop. It is a fun, easy lunch trip, and it pairs well with a stop in Ash Fork on the way home.
It is a small town, so check hours before you go, especially around Thanksgiving.

📖 Worth the Read
Before it was Prescott Valley, it was Lonesome Valley
A two-minute read
Long before there were neighborhoods here, there was open range. In December 1863 the U.S. Army brought 500 beef cattle and 700 working cattle into the territory, and ranching in Northern Arizona took off. Lonesome Valley, which takes in today’s Chino Valley, Prescott Valley and Dewey-Humboldt, was one big shared pasture where herds roamed together.
To sort them out, ranchers held a roundup, which in Spanish is a rodeo. They picked a captain, pooled money for the cook and the chuck bill, and sorted cattle by ear nicks and brands. By 1888 Prescott merchants were sponsoring a Cowboy Tournament with cash prizes, the event later renamed the Prescott Rodeo.
One ranch family left something you can still walk: the Fains, whose land became Fain Park in Prescott Valley. Prescott Audubon leads a bird walk there this Friday morning.

💡 Last week's answer
Watson Lake, down among the boulders of the Granite Dells, is one of the most photographed spots in Prescott. Who is it named after?
C. A U.S. senator from Indiana
Watson Lake is named for Sen. James Watson of Indiana, a principal investor in the Arizona Land and Irrigation Co. Granite Creek Dam was built in 1914, the lake appeared in 1915, and in 1916 it first sent irrigation water to Chino Valley through 40 miles of earthen canals. This one stumped everybody last week.
Source: J.J. McCormack, “Willow and Watson Lakes Reflect a Beautiful History,” Days Past, Sharlot Hall Museum
