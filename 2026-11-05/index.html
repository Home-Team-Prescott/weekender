A free night about Granite Mountain
A free night about Granite Mountain
The Natural History Institute on North Marina Street hosts a free film presentation and talk on Thursday, November 5, about the Granite Mountain Wilderness: its plants and wildlife, its fire history, and how the community helps protect it.
Stan Bindell presents, with guests from the Granite Mountain Hotshots Learning and Tribute Center, the Arizona Master Naturalists and Yavapai Firewise. Doors open at 6:30, the talk starts at 7:00. It is free, but space is limited and registration is required.

🚗 Worth the Drive
Bell Rock, the easy way into red rock country
South of Sedona on Route 179, inside 90 minutes
The Bell Rock Pathway runs alongside Route 179 just north of the Village of Oak Creek, with Bell Rock, Baby Bell Rock and Courthouse Butte rising right beside it. The Forest Service rates it an easy, family-friendly trail.
Most of the path has a wide, hard surface, with a few steep hills, and it climbs only 185 feet. It runs 3.5 miles one way, so walk as far as you like and turn around. There are restrooms at the north and south trailhead lots.
Getting there: take I-17 to the Sedona exit (#298), then Route 179 west through the Village of Oak Creek. The south trailhead is 8.3 miles in. Watch for horses and mountain bikes; they share the trail.

📖 Worth the Read
Doc Holliday, dentist, Montezuma Street
A two-minute read
In 1880 the federal census taker in Yavapai County wrote down a 28-year-old dentist named “J. H. Holladay.” The spelling was off. The man was Doc Holliday, renting a room in Richard Elliott’s boarding house on Montezuma Street, just above Gurley. One of his housemates was the acting governor of Arizona Territory.
Doc first came to town in November 1879, with Wyatt Earp passing through to stay with his brother Virgil, who had been elected Prescott’s constable the year before. One of only two verified photographs of Doc was taken by a Prescott photographer on Cortez Street. And the famous story of him winning $40,000 on Whiskey Row? In a 1932 letter to the Prescott Evening Courier, Anton Mazzanovich called it “apple sauce.”
A historical marker now stands where the boarding house was. It is an easy stop on a walk downtown.

Source: Bradley G. Courtney, “Doc Holliday in Prescott,” parts one to three, and Bill Lynam, “The Facts of Virgil Earp’s Connection to Prescott,” Days Past, Sharlot Hall Museum.
💡 Last week's answer
In December 1863, before there was a Prescott, the Army set up the first Fort Whipple. Where was it?
C. Near Del Rio Springs, about 20 miles north
The first Fort Whipple went up near Del Rio Springs, about 20 miles north of where Prescott would be, on December 23, 1863. It was too far from the gold panning on Lynx Creek and too far from timber, so the Army soon moved it south.
Source: Al Bates, “Remembered Names and Forgotten Faces of Fort Whipple,” Days Past, Sharlot Hall Museum
